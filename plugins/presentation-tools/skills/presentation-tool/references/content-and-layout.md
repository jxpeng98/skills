# Content depth and clear layouts

Read for a new deck, accompanying handout, or a requested improvement to substance,
template, or visual hierarchy. Apply the relevant parts; preserve the user's slide count,
format, style, and supplied design. A template reference conveys design; a file
provided only as a source of facts does not automatically dictate the design.

## Establish the content before arranging it

Identify what this audience needs to understand or decide and what the sources
actually support. Build a short internal outline linking each substantive slide
to its point, concrete support, explanation, and source. This is a working aid,
not an additional document the user must approve before creation continues.

- A result needs its value, units, comparison base, time window, and relevant
  uncertainty. Show why the comparison answers the slide's question.
- A mechanism needs actors or components, the important steps, and the reason
  those steps produce the outcome. A labelled example can make it understandable.
- A comparison needs consistent dimensions and meaningful differences; describe
  the cost, constraint, or failure case as well as the benefit.
- A proposal needs the concrete action, its rationale and tradeoff, and any
  supplied owner, timing, or acceptance condition. Mark unsupplied choices as
  proposals, not approvals or commitments.

Choose what serves this slide; do not require every slide to contain all four
forms. An explanatory deck does not need an invented recommendation. Keep enough
context for a reader unfamiliar with the source to understand the important terms
and relationships. Technical depth can be an annotated example or causal sequence,
not necessarily a numerical chart or more words.

Order ideas by what the audience needs to know next. Introduce an unfamiliar term
before relying on it, carry the same referent across slides, and show the step
between evidence and conclusion. A worked example should explain its setup,
important steps, and what the result demonstrates; do not just display an answer
or code block. Use this where it helps learning, not as a required slide sequence.

Write the explanation in natural target-language word order before shortening it
for the page. Keep the actor, action, and the scope of conditions or negation clear.
Preserve precise terms, but replace decorative abstractions with what actually
happens. If compression removes a necessary relationship, restructure the page
instead of leaving disconnected keywords or hiding the relationship in notes.

Keep the argument's essential evidence, explanation, and qualification on the
canvas. Speaker notes and appendices hold derivations, extra examples, or reading
detail, not information needed to make the visible claim honest. Preserve source
anchors such as document/table/page names in notes or appropriate source areas;
keep essential caveats visible. Verify calculations against the source.

When source material is insufficient, request the specific missing evidence if
the task depends on it. Continue supported portions. For an authorized conceptual
illustration, label it as an illustration; never disguise assumed data as results.
Do not add generic benefits or fetch unrelated statistics to fill blank space.

## Slides, speaker notes, and reading handouts

Honor the requested deliverables and their different reading conditions:

- **Slides:** show the claim or question, its essential support, and the relevant
  qualification at presentation scale. Use a diagram, comparison, or annotated
  example when it explains more clearly than prose; an asset still needs a clear
  relationship to the point.
- **Speaker notes:** add oral explanation, delivery cues, or supporting detail.
  Notes are not automatically a student handout, and the spoken account must not
  contradict or overstate the visible evidence.
- **Reading handout:** make the selected topic understandable without a speaker.
  Expand the steps compressed on slides into connected explanation, define terms
  at the reader's level, and include a worked example or derivation where useful.
  Retain source anchors, assumptions, and limits. Do not merely copy bullets,
  screenshots, or cues such as "explain this here". If the user requests a printout
  of the slides, preserve that output instead of turning it into a new textbook.

For a teaching handout, include practice or an answer explanation when requested
or useful for the learning purpose; do not invent assessment policy. Label invented
teaching examples as illustrative, distinct from supplied results. Match any page
or word limit by selecting and explaining the important content, not by shrinking
type or leaving reasoning unfinished. Use reading-oriented headings, paragraphs,
and page breaks; a slide grid is not automatically a readable document layout.

When multiple outputs are requested, check their shared examples, quantities,
terminology, conclusions, and caveats together. A handout may go deeper without
adding unsupported claims. Do not create extra outputs for a slide-only request.

## Define an actual template

Use supplied masters, layouts, branding, and aspect ratio. For a new design,
establish consistent margins, title position, type levels, content areas, source
placement, and restrained color roles. Set these in reusable masters, styles, or
layout components supported by the chosen tool, rather than drifting slide by slide.
Choose a small set of layouts according to the evidence:

| Content | Suitable composition | What makes it clear |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quantitative result | Chart plus a short interpretation | Label units, bases, time period, and the decisive comparison directly. |
| Options or before/after | Aligned columns or a comparison table | Use the same dimensions and show the relevant tradeoff. |
| Mechanism or workflow | Labelled sequence or native editable diagram | Make roles, inputs, transitions, and outputs understandable. |
| Technical example | Code, equation, or screenshot with annotations | Highlight the exact behavior or relationship, not an unexplained asset. |
| Decision or next step | Evidence-led recommendation or action list | Separate known facts, proposed choices, and unresolved dependencies. |

These are layout choices, not mandatory slide types. Avoid decorative cards,
ornamental charts, or repeated equal-size blocks when the content has a different
hierarchy. A cover can stay simple; it is not sufficient to validate a template.

Populate representative layouts with real material, including the longest title
and a dense comparison, chart, or technical example, before applying a new design
throughout. Check hierarchy, wrapping, whitespace, and reading order at normal
presentation scale. In a new 16:9 deck without a supplied style, roughly 32 pt
titles and 20–24 pt body text are useful starting points, not universal limits;
judge the actual script, screen, viewing distance, and information density.
Use appropriate CJK fonts for Chinese. Do not shrink everything until it fits.
First simplify the arrangement, remove repetition, or split a topic within the
allowed slide count; retain decision-critical detail and qualifications.

## Example: replace an empty benefit with an explained finding

Illustrative source: a four-week pilot processed 80 cases; median handling time
was 18 minutes before and 11 minutes during the pilot. Errors were 5 and 4 in the
respective 80-case groups. Staff used a prefilled form; assignment was not random.
These example values are not evidence for another deck.

Weak slide: **Efficiency improvements** — faster service, better quality,
streamlined processes.

Useful slide: **Median handling time fell from 18 to 11 minutes in the pilot**.
Show the two comparable values with units and group sizes. Explain that the pilot
used a prefilled form; do not claim it proved the cause of the reduction. Keep the
four-week duration and non-random assignment visible. The error counts do not
establish a reliable quality improvement; retain them as a limitation or on a
relevant comparison slide rather than claiming "better quality".

The improvement comes from the specific finding, comparison, mechanism context,
and limits. It does not require a longer slogan or a denser wall of text.

## Verify the substance and the rendered page

Read title and body together: can the audience identify the point, see its support,
and understand the relationship without inventing missing steps? A slide that
could fit an unrelated project unchanged probably needs specific evidence or
should be removed. Check that the deck progresses without redundant summaries.
For a reading handout, read it without the slides or speaker cues: can this reader
follow the explanation and work through the example? Check any equations or
calculations and cross-output consistency, not just coverage of topic headings.

Inspect rendered layouts for title/body/source hierarchy, understandable charts,
legible labels, adequate contrast, missing glyphs, cropping, and overflow. Fix the
affected content or layout and inspect again. Source review and a successful
export alone do not establish visual quality.
