# English Writing

Read for English output, including translations into English. Follow the requested
regional variety; otherwise follow the source consistently. Preserve quotations,
proper names, and established terminology rather than changing them to match a
spelling preference.

## Register and diction

- Everyday and professional prose should sound like a capable person addressing
  this reader: direct, fluent, and appropriately warm or restrained. Do not turn a
  work email into a journal abstract or an institutional notice into casual chat.
- Academic prose should follow disciplinary and venue conventions. Use precise
  claims and warranted qualification, with familiar syntax wherever possible.
  Do not inflate vocabulary or imply stronger evidence to sound authoritative.
- Prefer conventional collocations and concrete verbs. For example, `discuss the
  results` is usually more natural than `conduct a discussion concerning the
  results`. Keep a technical noun when it carries a distinction a plain verb loses.
- Use contractions, passive voice, and first person when the writer, genre, and
  meaning support them. None is universally required or forbidden. Preserve a
  passive construction when the actor is unknown or the result is the focus.

## Sentence and paragraph movement

- Keep the subject and main verb easy to locate. Untangle stacked modifiers,
  noun chains, and long interruptions without stripping useful qualification.
- Where it helps continuity, begin with a recognisable topic or referent and move
  towards the new point. Avoid ambiguous `this`, `it`, `they`, or `such`; name the
  finding, process, or person when the source makes that reference clear.
- Keep key terms stable across sentences. Repeating the correct term can be
  clearer than replacing it with a near-synonym that changes the referent.
- Give each paragraph a main purpose; place evidence, explanation, and limitations
  where they develop that purpose. Do not require every paragraph to use the
  same topic-sentence, three-points, concluding-sentence template.
- Let sentence length follow the idea. Combine closely related fragments and
  split overloaded sentences at meaningful boundaries. Do not force punchy
  fragments or uniformly short sentences to simulate natural writing.
- Across languages, rebuild clause order, emphasis, articles, prepositions, and
  idioms for English. Preserve meaning rather than the source's grammatical frame.

## Transitions

Use `however`, `although`, or `but` for a real contrast or counterexpectation;
`because`, `therefore`, or `as a result` only for a supported reason or consequence.
Use `also`, `moreover`, or `furthermore` when the added point needs that signal.
These are choices, not a blacklist. Often a repeated key noun, a clear subject,
or adjacent sentences supplies enough continuity.

When the topic changes, start an appropriately framed sentence or paragraph.
Do not use `moreover` to disguise an unrelated point or `therefore` to supply a
missing inference. Preserve a real limitation even when it interrupts good news.

Illustrations, not fixed replacement patterns:

- `The survey closed on Friday. However, it received 42 responses.`
  can become `The survey closed on Friday with 42 responses.` when no contrast is
  intended. Both facts remain; the false opposition disappears.
- `The new form was shorter, but users still needed help completing it.`
  already expresses a useful contrast; removing `but` is not an improvement.

## Final read

Check that the reader can follow who or what each sentence concerns, why the next
sentence follows, and how the paragraph advances the point. Recheck idiomatic
collocations and consistent spelling, tense, and register. Preserve necessary
connectors and caveats; remove only wording that distorts or obscures the thought.
